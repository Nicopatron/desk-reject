# The claim ladder

The rule R5 computes with, and the evidence it stands on. Read the limits at the bottom before citing anything here.

**This ladder is our own synthesis. It is not an established standard and is never presented as one.** No paper contains it. What the literature establishes is that the problem is real, prevalent, and driven by causal language — not this particular set of rungs. The rungs are a design tool, built to make one rule computable instead of felt. Their authority is that they are transparent, not that someone published them.

## The ladder

| Rung | The claim asserts | Example verbs |
|---|---|---|
| **L0 describe** | this happened, in these patients | *performed · observed · the rate was · we report* |
| **L1 associate** | these varied together | *associated with · correlated · linked to* |
| **L2 predict** | this forecasts that | *predicts · risk factor for · identifies patients who* |
| **L3 cause** | this produced that | *reduces · improves · superior to · safe · effective · enables* |

## The ceiling a design carries

| Declared design | Ceiling |
|---|---|
| Case series / single-arm, no control | **L0** |
| Retrospective comparative, control, unadjusted | **L1** |
| Retrospective/prospective comparative, adjusted for confounders | **L1** (L2 if the stated aim is prediction and it's validated) |
| RCT | **L3** |

**Why the ceiling sits there.** In a retrospective cohort nobody assigned the treatment for the study's sake — the surgeon chose, patient by patient, for reasons usually absent from the record. The healthier patient plausibly got the newer operation. So when the newer operation shows better outcomes, the design cannot separate *the operation worked* from *these patients were less sick to begin with*. Adjustment narrows that gap for the confounders you measured and does nothing for the ones you didn't. Randomisation is the only thing that takes the choice away from the surgeon, which is the only reason an RCT reaches L3.

A ceiling is not a demerit on the study. It is a statement of what the study is able to answer.

## The evidence

### Primary anchor — spin is measured, prevalent, and mostly causal language

**Lazarus C, Haneef R, Ravaud P, Boutron I.** *Classification and prevalence of spin in abstracts of non-randomized studies evaluating an intervention.* BMC Med Res Methodol. 2015 Oct 13;15:85. **PMID 26462565** · DOI 10.1186/s12874-015-0079-x · PMCID PMC4604617.

Verified against PubMed 2026-07-17 — the PubMed abstract record, which is not word-for-word identical to the PMC full text. Verbatim from it:

> "Among the 128 assessed articles assessed, 107 (**84%**) had at least one example of spin in their abstract. The most prevalent strategy of spin was the use of **causal language, identified in 68 (53%) abstracts**. Other frequent strategies were linguistic spin, inadequate implications for clinical practice, and lack of focus on harm, identified in 33 (26%), 25 (20%), and 34 (27%) abstracts respectively. **Abstract conclusions of 61 (48%) articles featured a high level of spin.**"

Its three levels, verbatim: high is "spin reported without uncertainty or recommendations for further trials", moderate "spin reported with some uncertainty **or** recommendations for further trials", low "spin reported with uncertainty **and** recommendations for further trials." The whole distinction rides on that *or* / *and*.

**Sample**: 128 non-randomized studies assessing an intervention, published in BioMed Central Medical Series journals, 1 Jan 2011 – 31 Dec 2013. Two trained methodologists, disagreements by consensus.

Why this anchor and not a general spin paper: the design it studies — **non-randomized, evaluating an intervention** — is the exact design of the abstracts this editor reads. The 84% is not an intuition about the craft. It is a count.

### Secondary anchor — the verb is a bad instrument, and reviewers heard cause anyway

**Haber NA, Wieten SE, Rohrer JM, Arah OA, Tennant PWG, Stuart EA, et al.** *Causal and Associational Language in Observational Health Research: A Systematic Evaluation.* Am J Epidemiol. 2022 Nov 19;191(12):2084-2097. **PMID 35925053** · DOI 10.1093/aje/kwac137 · PMCID PMC11043784.

Verified against PubMed 2026-07-17. Verbatim:

> "Reviewers rated the causal implication of exposure/outcome linking language as **none (no causal implication) in 13.8%, weak in 34.2%, moderate in 33.2%, and strong in 18.7%** of abstracts. **The implied causality of action recommendations was higher than the implied causality of linking sentences for 44.5% or commensurate for 40.3% of articles.** The most common linking word in abstracts was **'associate' (45.7%)**. Reviewers' ratings of linking word roots were highly heterogeneous; **over half of reviewers rated 'association' as having at least some causal implication.**"

That middle sentence is the one the recommendation rule rests on: in nearly half of articles, what the authors told you to *do* implied more cause than the sentence that linked exposure to outcome. It is why an action recommendation is read against the ceiling and not against the linking verb.

**Sample**: 1,170 articles screened from 18 high-profile journals (65 per journal), published 2010–2019. Three reviewers rating abstracts and full text.

## The tension this creates — stated, not buried

Haber's own conclusion, verbatim:

> "**This research undercuts the assumption that avoiding 'causal' words leads to clarity of interpretation in medical research.**"

Read plainly, that cuts against a naive reading of this ladder. If over half of reviewers hear a causal implication in the word *association* — the very word the ladder parks at L1 — then swapping *reduces* for *was associated with* does not deliver the honesty the swap appears to buy. The safe verb is not safe. Citing Haber's percentages while omitting that sentence would be spin, in a build whose whole subject is spin, and it would be the exact self-betrayal this editor is built to catch.

So it is load-bearing instead: **Haber is the strongest argument for R9's refusal.**

If a word swap actually fixed the problem, a rewriter would be the right tool — it would hand you the correct verb and you would be done. Haber measures that it doesn't. The verb is the *evidence that surfaces the claim*, not the claim, and not the fix. Which is precisely why this editor names the rung and refuses to hand you the replacement text: giving you a better word would relocate the overclaim, not remove it, and it would leave you convinced you'd dealt with it. The polish that makes bad science sound better is the failure mode; a verb swap dressed as a correction is that failure mode in miniature.

What the ladder therefore computes is **whether the design carries the claim** — not whether the sentence contains a bad word. The verb is where the check starts, never where it ends. What the author does about it is the author's decision, and there is more than one honest answer: narrow the claim, add the design that carries it, or state the ceiling and let the result stand at what it is.

## What this file does not establish

- **Neither source measures surgical congress abstracts.** Lazarus sampled BMC-series journal articles; Haber sampled 18 high-profile journals. Both are *published journal abstracts*. This editor reads *congress abstracts, before submission*. That the same failure appears in the same shape in that adjacent population is an **inference we are making**, not a finding either paper reports. It is a reasonable inference — same authors, same designs, same incentives, lighter review — and it is still an inference. Named, not smuggled.
- **Neither source contains this ladder.** Haber's four levels (none/weak/moderate/strong) grade *how strongly language implies cause* — a different axis from *what kind of claim is being made*. Lazarus's categories are spin *strategies* (causal language, linguistic spin, inadequate implications, lack of focus on harm) and *levels* (high/moderate/low). Neither maps to L0–L3. The mapping from design to ceiling is standard epidemiological reasoning; the tabulation of it here is ours.
- **No EQUATOR reporting guideline exists for conference abstracts of observational surgical studies.** Verified against the EQUATOR site: it lists PRISMA-DTA, STARD, PRISMA 2020, CONSORT — none covers this case. This ladder fills that gap as our synthesis. It does not fill it as a standard, and nothing in this folder should read as though a committee agreed to it.
- **The verb lexicons in `check.py` are ours too** — assembled for this build, in Spanish and English, not drawn from a validated instrument. They are visible in the script and arguable line by line, which is the only defence they have.
